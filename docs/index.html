Hunger

Knut Hamsun

Translated by George Egerton

Project Gutenberg

Knut Hamsun was a Nazi. He won the Nobel Prize in 1920, praised Hitler publicly, gave his Nobel medal to Joseph Goebbels, and wrote a fawning obituary for Hitler the day after his death. While his countrymen were imprisoned, tortured, and killed under German occupation, Hamsun cheered the occupiers on. He also wrote Hunger, the first novel set entirely inside the mind of its main character, a book that made Kafka, Beckett, and most of modern fiction possible.

Both of these things are true and neither cancels the other out.

Karl Ove Knausgaard, who named his own six-volume masterpiece My Struggle — the same title as Hitler's Mein Kampf — has written with unusual honesty about this problem. In Book One, he writes a mock epitaph for his own tombstone: "I'm no Nazi, but I like brown shirts / I write Gothic script until it hurts!" It is a joke, but it is not only a joke. In Book Six, he drops the comedy and describes watching footage of Nazi rallies and feeling "the sheer might of the uniformed, I-less community, the strength of the collective, and oh, how one might long to be part of such a we." He is not confessing sympathy. He is confessing susceptibility. He is saying: I am a product of my time and place, and I cannot be certain what I would have done in a different one. On Hamsun specifically, Knausgaard has said: "He was a full-blooded Nazi. We have to be honest about that." And: "He is not a Nazi in his writing. But in his essays and in his speeches, there's a big dissonance."

The jurist Carl Schmitt provided the legal framework that gave Hitler dictatorial power, justified political murder, and organized conferences to purge Jewish thought from German law. He never repented. Sartre, champion of freedom, took a teaching position vacated by a Jewish professor barred under Vichy racial laws, published under German censorship, and contributed to a German-funded magazine — then spent the rest of his life performing the role of the engaged intellectual. These are not equivalent sins, but they share a family resemblance: the ease with which brilliant minds accommodate the unforgivable.

This site does not exist to rehabilitate Hamsun. It exists because Hunger is a great novel, and reading it carefully — which is what these companion texts try to help you do — means holding the art and the life in the same hand without pretending either one is weightless. Most of our attention should go to the people who rise out of history stubbornly opposed to its darkness. Hamsun was not one of them. But the starving young writer he invented in 1890, stumbling through Christiania with nothing but his own consciousness for company — that character, at least, belongs to everyone.

The body lies to the mind, and the mind believes it. The starving man does not think, "I am light-headed from hunger and therefore feel grandiose." He thinks, "I am strong as a giant." Hamsun understood, decades before psychoanalysis had the vocabulary for it, that consciousness is not transparent to itself.

— from The Invention of the Interior
